Is that a lot? Usually, yes.

Most owners we show this to have never seen the four regimes added up in one number. It is bigger than they expect, and more of it is avoidable than they expect.

A single mid-size ship can carry a six-figure annual carbon bill.

Across a fleet that becomes the kind of number a board asks about. Three things usually drive it, and all three can be measured before you change anything:

  • Allowances you buy anyway. From 2026 you surrender for 100% of EU-scope emissions, and methane and nitrous oxide now count too.
  • A fuel penalty you may not have counted. Ordinary fuel oil sits above the FuelEU limit, so a shortfall accrues quietly all year.
  • A rating that drifts down while you do nothing. The CII limit tightens annually — the example above goes from C to E by 2030 without changing a thing.

If your number looks high, that is worth a conversation. We will tell you which part is real, which part is our estimate, and what it would take to move it. If there is nothing worth doing, we will say so.

What actually matters first

Not everything is equally urgent in 2026. This is the order we would work in, and why.

1
Cash, every year

EU ETS and UK ETS

Allowances you buy and surrender. From 2026 it is the full 100% of EU-scope emissions, with methane and nitrous oxide included. This is the largest single number for most fleets.

2
Cash, and a tradable position

FuelEU Maritime

A limit on the greenhouse-gas intensity of the energy you burn, not on your tonnes. Running conventional fuel puts you over it, and the shortfall is charged per tonne.

3
Rising every year to 2030

CII rating

No direct fine, but a poor rating has consequences: three years at D, or one year at E, requires a documented corrective plan — and charterers increasingly screen on it.

4
Mostly settled

EEXI

A one-off design check that the existing fleet cleared back in 2023. It matters again only for a newbuilding, a major conversion, or a change in engine power limitation — so we keep the check free rather than making it the headline.

How the numbers are worked out

No black box. Here is the actual arithmetic behind a CII rating — the rest follows the same pattern.

attained= CO2 emitted capacity × distance sailed

How much carbon the ship emits to move one tonne of capacity one mile.

required= (1 − Z)× a × capacity−c

a and c set the 2019 fleet average for that ship type; Z is how much tighter this year is. Beat the required figure and you rate well.

We check ourselves against your verifier — and show you the result

The public register carries each ship's EU-scope CO2 as that ship's accredited verifier calculated it. We rebuild that number independently and compare the two.

On a 400-ship sample we land on the exact same tonne for about two in three ships, and within a couple of percent for roughly four in five. Where it differs, the published figure usually covers only part of the year or part of the operator's obligation — detail the public record does not expose.

We show you that comparison on every vessel, so you can see which case yours is rather than take our word for it.

Every figure traces to a named regulation

Carbon factors, reference lines, reduction factors and fuel properties are taken from the IMO resolutions and EU regulations themselves, checked line by line against the published texts — not from secondary summaries.

No AI touches a compliance number. The calculations are ordinary code built from the rules, and we will walk your verifier through any of them.

What is an estimate, and what is not

Anything rebuilt from public data is an estimate and says so every time. Deadweight is not published, so a rating depends on the capacity you give us. Send one noon-report file and the same screen becomes your own verified position.
